guides: the privacy cluster, including the part we usually leave out

Three more guides, on the queries where the site's own positioning is the
answer. The results for these are unusually weak: "online tools that don't
upload your files" returns DEV.to and Medium posts plus unknown micro-domains
with no established brand anywhere, and "client side file conversion privacy"
is led by three separate developers' "I built this" show-and-tell posts.

The flagship page says the thing these pages almost never say. A site can
process your files locally and still load fonts, count a page view and talk to
an ad network — this one does — and "your file is never uploaded" and "this
page makes no network requests" are different claims. Conflating them is why a
reader who opens developer tools, sees requests, and concludes the whole claim
was marketing is being reasonable. Saying it plainly costs nothing and is the
only version of the argument that survives being checked, which is the entire
point of the section.

It also states the limits of the build check rather than waving at it. What
checkPrivacyClaims does is refuse to compile a browser-processed tool whose
code references fetch, XMLHttpRequest, WebSocket, EventSource or sendBeacon,
with a stricter storage rule for the two encryption tools. That is static
analysis of this project's own tool code, not a formal proof and not an audit
of every dependency. What it removes is the specific way these claims go
stale: someone adding a well-meaning "send this to our server" feature in two
years while the privacy page still says otherwise.

The converter guide is built on the FBI Denver warning of 7 March 2025, and on
the detail in it that actually changes behaviour: the malicious converters
work. They return a correct converted file — which is what keeps them from
being reported — while harvesting the original for social security numbers,
dates of birth, banking details, wallet information and passwords. So "it
worked" is evidence about the conversion and nothing else, and the obvious
safety test most people apply is the one test these sites are built to pass.

The JWT guide treats the token as what it is: a bearer credential, where
possession is the whole of the authorisation. Decoding needs no server at all —
the header and payload are base64url, not encrypted — so a decoder that posts
your token somewhere has no technical reason to, and has just been handed a
working credential. Cited to RFC 7519 and RFC 6750 rather than to a blog.

Two fixes found while wiring it up: a guide may cite a page on this site (the
privacy page, which these guides make claims about), and both the rendered link
and the schema.org citation handled that wrongly. Relative URLs in JSON-LD are
not resolvable by a consumer holding the graph alone, so citations are now
absolutised; and internal sources render as ordinary links rather than
nofollow-and-new-tab, which would have discarded an internal link and opened a
tab to go nowhere.

check-seo caught an over-length description twice and a primary keyword absent
from both metaTitle and metaDescription. Fixed rather than waived.
